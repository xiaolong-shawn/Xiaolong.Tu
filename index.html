About

Phones, cars and cameras now run neural networks all day, but they run on batteries. My work asks a question the field usually skips: how much energy does a model actually spend, and where does it go?

To answer it I build measurement tools — kernel-level energy predictors, runtime latency profilers for on-device language models, automated power-profiling rigs — and then use what they reveal to search for architectures that keep accuracy while spending far fewer joules. Lately that lens has moved up the stack: even the wording of a prompt turns out to have a measurable energy signature on a phone.

The goal behind all of it is sustainability: edge AI that stays useful without quietly running up a carbon bill. My interests sit at the intersection of edge computing, energy-efficient AI systems and sustainable AI.

Before returning to research I spent more than ten years as an engineer, shipping products at Ericsson, Huawei, Qualcomm and Apple. That decade is why I care about numbers taken from real hardware rather than from a simulator.

What I work on

Measurement

Seeing where the energy goes

High-resolution power and latency profiling on commodity phones, broken down by inference phase and by individual kernel, with almost no overhead on the workload being measured.

Prediction

Estimating cost before deployment

Kernel-level predictors that estimate the energy of an unseen network on a given device, and transfer to new hardware from a handful of calibration samples instead of a full re-measurement.

Design

Searching for efficient models

Hardware-aware neural architecture search driven by real device energy, using Pareto-based multi-objective search to trade accuracy against power deliberately rather than by accident.
